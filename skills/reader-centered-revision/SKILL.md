# Reader-centered revision

Use this skill when revising prose that is grammatically acceptable but unclear, abstract, repetitive, hard to follow, or poorly organized. Use it mainly after a draft exists. The writer keeps control of meaning and final wording.

## Procedure

1. State the reader's task and the point of the passage. Write down what the reader must understand next, then verify that the draft supports that path.

2. Identify the main characters and actions. Put the important actors in grammatical subjects and express their important actions with verbs when that makes the sentence easier to understand. Replace a nominalization with an action when it hides who does what.

3. Check sentence openings and endings. Put familiar or already established information near the start. Put the important new or complex information near the end, where readers give it more attention.

4. Check the main clause. Bring the main verb close to its subject. Shorten long introductions, abstract subjects, and interruptions when they delay the point.

5. Check cohesion within each paragraph. Keep the paragraph's topic visible through consistent subjects or terms. Add a plain transition when the relationship between sentences is not clear.

6. Check paragraph and document structure. Start a section with its purpose or topic, then add support in an order the reader can follow. Confirm that the larger document motivates the reader, frames the problem, and keeps its main line visible.

7. Remove words that do no work. Cut repeated ideas, filler, and obvious implications. Compress long phrases when the shorter form keeps the meaning. Prefer an affirmative form when it is clearer, but keep a negative form when the distinction matters.

8. Control sentence growth. Split sentences with several nested relationships when the reader must hold too many relationships at once. Keep a longer sentence when its structure represents one connected idea clearly.

9. Review the result as a reader. Check whether each sentence says who acts, what happens, what is familiar, and what is new. Preserve technical precision and the writer's intended voice.

10. Use an LLM as a diagnostic reviewer when useful. Ask it to flag hidden actions, abstract subjects, weak links, repetition, filler, and order problems. Ask for reasons or marked passages, not replacement prose. Compare the original and proposed changes, and accept only revisions the writer can explain and endorse.

## Boundaries

- Treat the rules as ways to predict reader responses, not as absolute bans.
- Do not remove a passive construction, technical term, long sentence, or negative sentence only because a rule mentions it.
- Do not use the LLM to choose the final voice or wording.
- Stop when the reader can follow the meaning without unnecessary work and further changes would only change style preference.

## Verification

Read the revised passage once for meaning and once for reader effort. Confirm that the revision preserves the claim, evidence, qualifications, and intended emphasis.
