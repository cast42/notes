### Extracted passages and claims

The opening article argues that a quality and ethical approach is grounded in multidimensional critical thinking and is “unbound from a mechanical process.” It uses transcripts as an example: transcripts can distance the researcher from the reality of an interview or group discussion, and a thoughtful analysis should draw on more than the written transcript. The text asks researchers to reflect on what was and was not said, how the researcher influenced what was learned, which contextual inputs shaped the result, and what connections exist between non-obvious but related ideas.

The compilation contrasts this with a mechanical approach. It cites Braun and Clarke’s description of analysis as immersion, reading, reflecting, questioning, imagining, wondering, writing, retreating, and returning. It warns against importing linear assumptions from quantitative analysis when interpreting causes, consequences, and relationships in qualitative data.

The discussion of fairness says that researchers owe participants a multidimensional approach that fully incorporates their contributions. A “fair voice” involves sample scope, inclusion, researcher skill, ongoing reflexivity, and quality strategies that embrace diversity. The text gives special attention to participants with weak or marginalized voices and asks researchers to examine how their assumptions, values, biography, and social or economic position shaped questions, interjections, listening, and behaviour.

The design article presents the Total Quality Framework (TQF) as paradigm-neutral, flexible, and applicable across research phases. It connects the framework to trustworthiness criteria such as credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Thick description enables readers to judge whether findings can transfer to other contexts; an audit trail supports dependability; and the evidence behind interpretations supports confirmability.

The TQF article says the framework is not intended to produce a binary valid/invalid or useful/not-useful verdict. Instead it helps users form a degree of confidence—from not at all confident to extremely confident—about validity and usefulness. It is presented as a way to identify threats at each stage of research and to empower readers to make their own decisions about how much weight to give findings.

The four TQF components are:

- Credibility: data collection.
- Analyzability: processing, analysis, and verification.
- Transparency: reporting enough detail, including thick description and an audit trail.
- Usefulness: whether the outcomes answer the research question and serve a valuable purpose.

The case example applies the TQF to limitations in a doctoral study based on 38 semi-structured interviews with climate and energy-systems modellers. It organizes possible weaknesses around sampling and data gathering, data processing and verification, transparency, and usefulness.

The interview case study describes an 86-interview study and lists practices intended to improve credibility: stratifying participants, screening for qualified participants, building rapport, using a funnel-shaped guide, allowing flexible scheduling, clarifying the sponsor’s role, and keeping a reflexive journal. The journal records the interviewer’s thoughts and observations about both interviewer and participant conduct.

The mixed-methods article says the four TQF components should be adapted to the connection between qualitative and quantitative data rather than treating the qualitative part as an isolated component. It proposes checking contradictions and variation within qualitative data and between qualitative and quantitative data, and asking how prior quantitative knowledge shaped the interviewer’s questions, interjections, listening, and behaviour.
