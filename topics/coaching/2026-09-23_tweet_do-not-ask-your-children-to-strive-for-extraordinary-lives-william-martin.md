# Do not ask your children to strive for extraordinary lives — William Martin

*Reads with Ravi — @readswithravi*

## TL;DR

- William Martin’s parenting poem argues against making extraordinary achievement the central goal of childhood.
- The positive alternative is to make ordinary life vivid: taste, touch, grief, attention and everyday wonder.
- The message is not anti-effort; it shifts the target from status and exceptional outcomes toward presence and a grounded life.

## Highlights

- Do not ask your children to strive for extraordinary lives.
- Help children discover the wonder and marvel of an ordinary life.
- The extraordinary will take care of itself.

## What sticks

The poem is a correction to achievement-first parenting. It does not argue that children should avoid effort, competence or ambitious projects. It questions making exceptional status the organising purpose of childhood. The alternative is to make ordinary life vivid enough that children can develop attention, affection, grief, gratitude and a capacity for delight.

The examples matter because they are concrete rather than motivational abstractions: taste fruit, feel a hand, cry when people or animals die. The poem treats emotional range and sensory attention as part of a good life, not as distractions from achievement.

The final claim is deliberately open-ended. “The extraordinary will take care of itself” can mean that meaningful achievement may emerge from a grounded life; it need not mean that achievement is guaranteed or that every child should become exceptional. The durable lesson is about what parents choose to reward and notice every day.

## Context

The image attributes the poem to William Martin. It is commonly identified with *The Parent’s Tao Te Ching: Ancient Advice for Modern Parents*, first published in 1999. Martin’s book applies Taoist ideas to parenting; this note records the poem’s principle rather than treating the social-media image as a complete account of the book.
