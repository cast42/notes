# Does Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment?

## TL;DR

A 2016 meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies (19,951 participants) found that interventions encouraging people to monitor progress improved goal attainment on average (standardized effect **d = 0.40**, 95% CI 0.32–0.48). For coaching, a lightweight, client-chosen record and regular reflection is a plausible practice—not a guarantee or a substitute for addressing obstacles outside the client's control.

## What the research says

- Harkin and colleagues compared interventions designed to increase progress monitoring with control conditions. The pooled effect is an average standardized difference across varied goals and measures, **not** a 40% increase or a personalized probability of success.
- Across studies, effects were larger where progress was recorded and where it was reported or made public. These are **between-study moderator comparisons**, not proof that adding a written log or public disclosure caused the difference: interventions differed in other ways, and the authors note that moderators may be correlated.
- The review found that monitoring behavior was more closely associated with changes in behavior, while monitoring outcomes was more closely associated with outcome changes. This suggests choosing measures that match the target, rather than assuming any metric will do.
- Much of the evidence concerned health; the authors noted sparse data for non-health goals. Applying the result to professional or executive coaching is an informed extrapolation.
- Some interventions combined monitoring with other techniques. The pooled result does not isolate the effect of journaling alone, explain precisely why it works, or establish handwriting as better than typing.

## Try it in coaching

**Collaborative experiment, not a prescribed protocol:**

1. Agree on an outcome that matters to the client and one small, observable action plausibly within their control. For example, for better delegation: initiate two delegation conversations this week; separately note whether responsibilities became clearer.
2. Choose a low-friction record and a review rhythm together: a private note, spreadsheet, or paper log. Capture the action, relevant outcome, and context in a sentence or two.
3. At the next session ask: “What did you notice? What helped or got in the way? What does this suggest changing?” Treat missed actions as information, not a character judgment.
4. Adjust the action or measure, or stop tracking if it adds burden, distorts priorities, or does not help. Sharing with the coach is optional and client-directed; public disclosure is not necessary.

**Example log:** “Tuesday: delegated the client briefing. The deadline was clear; ownership of follow-up was not. Next time, agree on who follows up before the meeting ends.”

## Post versus paper

[Ed Latimore’s post](https://x.com/EdLatimore/status/2119556238803366192) highlights the review and recommends writing down both goals and steps. His suggestion that handwriting might be slightly better is explicitly intuition, not a finding established by this meta-analysis. His description that it “dramatically” improves the odds goes beyond what the standardized average effect establishes for an individual.
