# Technical career advice: coaching prompts from Cassie Kozyrkov

## TL;DR

Kozyrkov's post points to a collection of her articles on technical careers: interviews, impostor feelings, selecting employers and projects, communication, motivation and goals. For coaching, use these as **questions to investigate with the client**, not prescriptions: the right answer depends on the person, the workplace and their agency.

## Coaching applications

The prompts below are coaching adaptations, not Kozyrkov's quoted questions or an assertion that every linked article recommends them.

1. **Difficult interview questions and boundaries.** Rehearse answering the substance honestly while recognizing that a client need not disclose everything. Ask: “Which question worries you? What can you say truthfully, and what would you rather keep private?” Kozyrkov's [interview article](https://kozyrkov.medium.com/how-to-deal-with-tricky-questions-2fc3103ab5a8) discusses privacy and redirecting awkward questions; dodging a legitimate job requirement can damage trust, so practice transparent boundaries rather than evasion.
2. **Impostor feelings versus a real development gap.** Ask: “What evidence supports your self-assessment? What feedback would distinguish an unfamiliar task from a genuine skill gap?” Normalize uncertainty without dismissing poor treatment or assuming every concern is impostor syndrome.
3. **Interviewing the employer.** Ask: “What would success look like in six months? Who makes decisions and supplies the data or resources? What is an example of the team acting on this work?” These are suggested coaching questions, **not** the three questions in Kozyrkov's separately linked article, which were not verified for this note.
4. **Career resilience.** Separate a job title's popularity from portable skills and actual opportunities. Ask: “Which skills and relationships would still be useful if your current role changed? What small step would increase your options?” Avoid promising job safety.
5. **Stakeholders and project health.** Explore who benefits, who decides and what incentives may affect the project. Ask: “Whose problem is this, who can act on the result, and what evidence would change your view?” Kozyrkov's [who/why/how framing](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kozyrkov_before-you-ask-why-ask-who-activity-7047675809561501696-Wx5q) emphasizes incentives; check interpretations with people instead of assuming bad motives.
6. **Communication and speaking.** Identify the audience's decision and prior knowledge before choosing detail. Ask: “What should this audience understand or do afterwards? Which example conveys it without unnecessary notation?” Adapt depth to the audience; equations can be appropriate when they help.
7. **Motivation and goals.** Treat strategies as personal experiments. Ask: “What goal matters to you, what has helped or hindered you before, and what small experiment could you try before our next session?” Kozyrkov's [motivation article](https://kozyrkov.medium.com/how-to-hack-your-motivation-according-to-a-decision-scientist-b948c2b4049e) stresses individual differences and testing what works. Avoid framing structural constraints or exhaustion as a mere motivation problem.

## A short session pattern

Let the client select the live career decision → explore their own success criteria and constraints → choose one relevant theme above → agree one client-owned experiment and a follow-up signal. For example, before an interview the experiment could be to ask the employer one concrete question about how decisions are made, then reflect on both the answer and how it was given.
