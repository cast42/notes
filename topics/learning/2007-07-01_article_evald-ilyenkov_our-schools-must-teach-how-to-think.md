# Our Schools Must Teach How to Think!

## TL;DR

Evald Ilyenkov argues that acquiring facts and developing the ability to think are neither identical nor separable: knowledge is necessary, but merely repeating it does not guarantee judgment. Learners need to grapple with meaningful problems, understand the questions concepts answer, and exercise judgment about when and how to apply them. His argument is a philosophical and political account of education, not a controlled evaluation of teaching methods.

## Core argument

- **Knowledge is necessary but not self-applying.** Memorizing a formula or rule is different from recognizing whether it fits a particular situation, using it, or knowing what to do when it does not. Ilyenkov does not argue for thinking without knowledge. (pp. 9–11) [essay]
- **Thinking develops in social activity.** He rejects the idea that ability is simply a fixed natural gift, stressing cultural inheritance, interactions, games, practical activity and educational conditions. This is his philosophical position; his sweeping claims about nature and development should not be mistaken for contemporary empirical findings. (pp. 11–12) [essay]
- **Present knowledge as an answer to a question.** Instead of starting and ending with definitions to reproduce, show the difficulty that generated a concept, give learners problems to work on, and connect general propositions to cases. He values learners formulating questions and understanding how a result was reached. (pp. 16–18) [essay]
- **Judgment must be exercised, not merely prescribed.** General rules and formal logic cannot independently decide their own applicability. Teachers also face a gap between general instructional principles and particular students and situations. The essay explicitly says philosophy alone cannot provide a complete ready-made teaching recipe. (pp. 10–11, 48–49) [essay]
- **Contradiction and dialectic matter to his view.** He asks learners to articulate competing considerations and examine the concrete situation rather than remove disagreement through verbal maneuvering. His theory is explicitly Marxist and Hegelian; dialectic is central to the argument, not merely a modern synonym for generic critical thinking. [essay]
- **Mathematics illustrates his critique.** In a lengthy discussion of number and introductory arithmetic, he argues that presenting number only as a property of counted collections narrows the idea and obscures measurement and relations between quantities. This is his proposed conceptual analysis, not proof that one particular curriculum works better. [essay]

## Reading critically

The essay is forceful and historically situated. Its claims that rote instruction can permanently damage thinking, and its broad claims about innate differences, go beyond evidence established in this essay. It is reasonable to distinguish **meaningless cramming** from all memorization: retrieval and practice can support later reasoning; whether an instructional method improves understanding or transfer requires empirical assessment. Nor does problem-solving automatically work well without sufficient knowledge and guidance.

There is productive overlap with [Willingham’s critical-thinking article](2008-03-01_article_daniel-willingham_critical-thinking-hard-to-teach.md): both reject the idea that rules alone make a thinker and connect thought to subject knowledge. Their emphases differ: Ilyenkov foregrounds concept formation, social practice and dialectic; Willingham uses cognitive research to stress domain knowledge and the difficulty of transfer. Their agreement does not make their theories or teaching prescriptions identical.

For AI-assisted work, one possible application—not a conclusion tested in the essay—is to preserve opportunities for a learner to formulate the problem and work through why an answer fits before an agent supplies polished results. This connects with [writing and learning](2026-10-01_article_pedro-de-bruyckere_writing-thinking-learning.md).
