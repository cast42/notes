# Critical Thinking: Why Is It So Hard to Teach?

## TL;DR

Daniel T. Willingham argues that critical thinking is not a general-purpose procedure that can be learned once and applied equally well to any situation. Reasoning depends on what the thinker knows about the subject, and on practice recognizing when and how to use that knowledge. General prompts can help cue thought, but they cannot replace domain knowledge or practice.

## Core argument

People often think about a problem through its **surface structure**—the story or setting—rather than its **deep structure**, the underlying concept or solution. This makes transfer difficult: a learner may solve a problem in a familiar form yet fail to recognize the same reasoning when it appears in a new context.

Willingham illustrates this with cited research. In one example, only 19% of participants recognized that a band-marching problem required the same mathematical solution as an earlier garden problem. He also describes problems whose solution depends on familiar stories: American and Chinese students performed differently on story-framed problems, and the pattern reversed when the cultural story changed. These examples illustrate how prior knowledge and context affect recognition; they are not evidence of a fixed national difference in reasoning.

The article does not say that teaching thinking strategies is useless. Metacognitive cues—such as looking for alternative explanations or checking evidence against an initial conclusion—can make critical thought more likely. But knowing that one should use a strategy is different from having the subject knowledge needed to carry it out.

## Teaching implications

- Teach reasoning within meaningful subject content, not as a content-free add-on.
- Give learners the knowledge needed to perform the reasoning move. For example, telling students to design a controlled experiment does not teach them which variables must be kept constant.
- Practise the same underlying idea across varied examples. Changing the surface details can help learners notice the deep structure and use it in new cases.
- Use metacognitive prompts as cues, paired with worked examples, feedback, and repeated practice.
- Assess transfer with unfamiliar examples. Success on the exact kinds of problems used in instruction does not establish broad transfer.

## Reading stance and limits

This is a research-informed 2008 article, not a new experiment or a current systematic review. Its memorable claim that critical thinking “is not a skill” is best read as a warning against assuming context-free transfer, not as a denial that people can develop specialized reasoning skills. Willingham notes that expertise can make some reasoning more skill-like within a domain. The durable point is that strategy instruction alone is insufficient: knowledge, cues, practice, and feedback work together.
