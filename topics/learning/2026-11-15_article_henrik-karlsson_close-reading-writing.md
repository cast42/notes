# You learn more about writing from close reading than from writing advice

## TL;DR

Henrik Karlsson argues that a writer can learn more by repeatedly studying a text that achieves a desired effect than by applying generic writing maxims. Close reading produces a personal, provisional toolkit suited to one's own projects and taste.

## The argument

- Abstract advice is a rough map: it may omit context, and its original destination may differ from yours. A rule that helps one piece can mislead another.
- Choose an example that does particularly well what you want to learn. Read it slowly, more than once, with a pencil; ask how it produces its effect.
- Form hypotheses and notice possible techniques, but hold them lightly: they are interpretations based on limited reading, not universal laws.
- The payoff is not merely a list of rules but a more detailed feel for the territory and tools meaningful to your own work.

## Example and open question

Karlsson rereads David Foster Wallace's reporting on John McCain's 2000 presidential campaign. He initially wants to understand how reportage and scene-setting are integrated with reflection, but becomes especially interested in how detailed descriptions sustain narrative momentum. **The publicly accessible introduction poses this question; the detailed analysis is subscriber-only and was not available for this note.** It would be misleading to infer his answer from the introduction.

## Try it

Pick a passage that achieves an effect you want. Read it twice, marking the places where your attention changes; write down a tentative explanation, then test one observed technique in a draft of your own. This is an application of the article's argument, not a prescribed procedure from its author.
