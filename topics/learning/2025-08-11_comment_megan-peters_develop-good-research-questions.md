# How to develop good research questions

## TL;DR

Megan A. K. Peters describes question formation as iterative creative work, not just a checklist for novelty and feasibility. Start with curiosity, build and critique connections, revisit close prior work through counterfactuals, and develop the question alongside a feasible approach and competing explanations. This is an expert **Comment**, not an empirical evaluation of the proposed process.

## Four phases

1. **Self-critical brainstorming.** Follow a persistent curiosity and generate connections without demanding polished ideas. Peters likes to talk aloud while walking, optionally transcribing her thoughts; then she challenges her own arguments and seeks perspectives from people who understand the field without being narrow specialists.
2. **Build context and connections.** Read deeply in the relevant literature and broadly across fields. Record not just summaries but connections, emerging questions and reasons papers are central or peripheral. Similar underlying problem structures can appear in superficially different fields.
3. **Distill the essence.** Return to the closest papers and ask what would change with another population, control, task, measurement or analysis. Generate multiple alternatives before choosing; they can expose competing explanations and promising questions.
4. **Pair the question with an approach.** Choose a focused question in light of knowledge, resources and expertise. Specify a way to investigate it and consider confounds and alternative interpretations that the design should rule out—or clearly acknowledge.

The stages are not a one-way funnel: new reading or criticism can justify returning to an earlier phase or abandoning the initial question.

## Four traps

- **A hypothesis is not mandatory for every question.** Descriptive and exploratory work can be valuable; specify the purpose and expectations appropriate to the question rather than forcing a predicted effect.
- **Sunk-cost attachment.** Time already invested does not make a weak question worth pursuing.
- **Someone had a similar idea.** Examine the overlap and whether a meaningful difference exists; do not assume either complete duplication or automatic novelty.
- **Hammer and nail.** Expertise is useful, but the familiar method is not necessarily the best fit for the question.

## Using AI without outsourcing the question

The [post](https://x.com/SciencNews/status/2120120357172727930) suggests the article might make a good AI prompt. Peters herself uses an LLM to organize spoken brainstorms but warns that summaries can be wrong; she cautions against relying on automated tools to make all the connections in a literature review. The point is to **support the researcher’s thinking**, not replace it.

**Adaptation, not the author’s prompt:** First write your own rough question and why it matters. Then ask an assistant: “Help me develop this question. Separate my assumptions from evidence; suggest relevant and cross-disciplinary literature to verify; propose alternative framings and ways to challenge nearby studies; identify confounds and feasible approaches. Ask me for my own connections before offering yours. Do not invent citations.” Check cited sources and retain the right to reject the framing. For a serious study, independent expert critique and actual literature review remain essential.
