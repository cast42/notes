# Why books don’t work

## TL;DR

Andy Matuschak argues that explanatory nonfiction often leaves readers with an illusion of understanding: finishing the text is not the same as being able to recall, explain, or use its ideas. His design challenge is to create media whose ordinary use supports the thinking, feedback, and practice that learning requires—not simply add more information.

## Key takeaways

- **The claim is scoped:** the target is detailed knowledge from explanatory nonfiction, not the enjoyment, inspiration, or other value of fiction and reading generally. Matuschak explicitly says he found no formal studies measuring how much of a nonfiction book readers retain; his broad prevalence claim is an inference from experience.
- **Transmission is an inadequate model:** clear exposition supplies material, but understanding depends on the learner engaging with it. His analogy is a lecture that feels clear until the student attempts the problems.
- **The hidden burden is metacognition:** readers must notice confusion, connect ideas, pose questions, choose strategies, and check their own understanding. Doing this while learning unfamiliar content is difficult and effortful.
- **Textbooks do more, but are not a complete answer:** exercises help, yet readers still decide what to do when stuck and whether they grasped the intended lesson. Courses can also supply sequencing, feedback, social context, accountability, and emotional engagement.
- **Design the medium around learning:** rather than assume a book’s familiar form is fixed, build its default interactions around evidence-informed learning activities. The author treats the larger design problem as an open research question.

## Quantum Country: an initial experiment

Matuschak and Michael Nielsen’s [Quantum Country](https://quantum.country/) interleaves short explanations with recall questions and later spaced reviews. The proposed mechanism is to strengthen memory for prerequisites and reveal gaps as readers progress. It illustrates a possible medium; **memory is only one part of understanding**, not proof of reasoning, transfer, or deep comprehension.

## Implications and limits

- After reading something important, try explaining an idea from memory, applying it to a new problem, and checking the result—not just rereading or saving a summary.
- Support should fit the learner: prompts and feedback can help, but creating one’s own questions can itself be productive, especially with adequate prior knowledge. A more interactive medium is not automatically better for every purpose.
- The essay presents an argument and research agenda, not an experiment establishing that books usually fail or that its proposed media outperform books in all contexts.
