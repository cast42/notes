# The Next China Shock Is Here — Brad Setser

## TL;DR

Brad Setser’s central argument is that the world may be entering a **China Shock 2.0**. The first shock was China’s rise as a low-cost exporter of clothing, furniture, appliances, and other goods in the 2000s. The new shock reaches advanced manufacturing—electric vehicles, batteries, solar panels, machinery—and potentially software and AI. That makes it more consequential for high-wage economies whose manufacturing sectors also support regional employment, research, and innovation.

## Key takeaways

- **The sector has moved up the value chain.** The concern is no longer only cheap imports displacing low-end factories; it is Chinese competition in industries treated as strategic and technologically advanced.
- **China’s domestic model matters.** The discussion connects high savings, state-directed finance, and industrial policy to the ability to sustain investment and export capacity.
- **The 2021 property collapse is a turning point in the framing.** Setser presents the post-property economy as relying more heavily on manufacturing and external demand.
- **Europe is especially exposed.** Losing automotive and other advanced manufacturing can remove not only jobs but also an ecosystem for research, engineering, and future innovation. Workers displaced by Chinese exports may not simply move into another export industry if China is not importing more from them.
- **Trade policy is a systems problem.** The episode examines currency policy, national-security supply chains, tariffs, leverage, and the tension between interdependence and decoupling rather than treating tariffs as a complete solution.

## The central idea

The important shift is from a **consumer-price shock** to a **productive-capacity shock**. China is not merely supplying the world with cheaper versions of mature goods; it is building dominant capacity in sectors that other countries expect to anchor their own growth, innovation, and strategic autonomy. The effect therefore propagates through local labour markets, industrial ecosystems, trade balances, supply-chain dependence, and domestic politics.

This is also why “just move workers into services” is an incomplete response. Advanced manufacturing is not only an employment category: it can be a platform for R&D, supplier learning, engineering capabilities, and regional economic coordination. Replacing its output with imports may improve consumer prices while weakening the capabilities that produce future innovation.
