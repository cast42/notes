# The Techtonic Shift — what reviewers are saying

## TL;DR

- The early public reaction is strongly positive, but it consists mainly of publisher-selected endorsements rather than independent reviews.
- Reviewers and institutional commentators praise the book as timely, analytically clear, and important for understanding how technology now shapes democratic and geopolitical power—especially Europe’s position.
- The core argument is that the post-Cold-War digital order is fragmenting into competing power blocs while democratic institutions have failed to adapt to Big Tech, AI, and strategic technology dependence.
- As of 5 September 2026, major retailer pages show no reader reviews, so a balanced critical consensus has not formed yet.

## Highlights

- Rob de Wijk calls the argument compelling and says it offers an important perspective on Europe’s failure to adapt institutions to technology’s effect on democracy.
- Marietje Schaake presents the book as essential for understanding how technology challenges governance and the task this creates for Europe.
- Virginia Dignum praises the historical depth and analytical clarity of the claim that technology is political and values are never neutral.
- The ICC Netherlands interview is not an independent review, but it reveals the book’s provocative policy direction: Europe should seek strategic autonomy and make difficult choices among the United States, China, and its own institutional priorities.
- The University of Amsterdam launch text reinforces the book’s central framing of technology as an instrument of geopolitical power and a pressure on democratic institutions.

## My notes

- The honest verdict is **positive launch reception, not yet a review consensus**. The three public endorsements are relevant and specific, but they are publisher-selected; the independent question is whether later reviewers accept the diagnosis and find the proposed European responses realistic.
- The strongest shared signal is that the book gives readers a useful political frame: technology is not neutral infrastructure, and dependence on foreign technology can become dependence in security, economic, and democratic decision-making.
