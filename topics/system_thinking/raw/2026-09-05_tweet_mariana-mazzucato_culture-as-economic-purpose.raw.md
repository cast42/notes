# Research capture: Mariana Mazzucato on culture as economic purpose

## Original post — 5 September 2026

Mazzucato says that arts and culture should be brought to the centre of the
economy rather than treated as peripheral. She presents art as an investment
and points readers to UCL's Public Value of Arts and Culture programme.

Source: [original X post](https://x.com/MazzucatoM/status/2096169007485272309)

## Follow-up reply

In a reply about 53 minutes later, Mazzucato sharpens the claim: an economics
that puts culture at the centre must change what counts as value, how culture
is funded, and who shares in the rewards it creates. She says culture is not
one instrument among many; it is how an economy imagines its goals. She points
to her book *The Common Good Economy* as a compass for this approach.

Source: [follow-up reply](https://x.com/MazzucatoM/status/2096178777533370439)

## Linked UCL framework

UCL describes PVAC as an applied research programme led by Mazzucato. It treats
arts and culture as essential long-term public investments and social
infrastructure. Its stated focus includes imagination, policy design, and
valuation. The programme argues that narrow direct-market metrics miss culture's
effects on public purpose, democratic participation, social cohesion, health,
education, innovation, skills, place, identity, and communities.

Source: [UCL Public Value of Arts and Culture](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/bartlett/public-purpose/policy/public-value-arts-and-culture-pvac)

## Linked book context

Mazzucato's *The Common Good Economy* argues that governments and businesses
should build purposeful economic relationships and shape markets toward
collective goals, rather than merely correct market or government failures.
The culture thread extends this by asking how societies imagine and define the
goals that economic policy should serve.

Source: [The Common Good Economy](https://marianamazzucato.com/books/the-common-good-economy/)

## Synthesis

The new move is to treat culture as **constitutive infrastructure**. The
argument is stronger than “culture has positive spillovers”: cultural activity
helps determine the ends, measures, legitimacy, and distributional rules of the
economic system. The practical consequences—new valuation methods, funding
models, and reward-sharing arrangements—are proposed research and policy work,
not established results.
