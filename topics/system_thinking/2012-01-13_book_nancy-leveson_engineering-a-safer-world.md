# Engineering a Safer World: Systems Thinking Applied to Safety

## TL;DR

Nancy Leveson argues that traditional safety methods built around component
failures and linear event chains are inadequate for complex, software-intensive
sociotechnical systems. She introduces STAMP, which models safety as a control
problem: accidents occur when safety constraints are inadequately enforced
across technical, human, organizational, and regulatory levels.

## Key takeaways

- **Safety is not the same as reliability.** A system can suffer an accident
  even when every component behaves as specified, because unsafe interactions
  can emerge between otherwise reliable parts.
- **Accidents are systemic.** Analysis should include control structures,
  feedback, process models, communication, coordination, organizational
  incentives, and change over time—not only a chain of proximate events.
- **STAMP is the causality model.** It represents safety through hierarchical
  control and the enforcement of constraints throughout a system.
- **STPA is the proactive analysis method.** It identifies unsafe control
  actions and the scenarios that could produce them, supporting hazard analysis
  and safety-guided design before losses occur.
- **CAST is the retrospective analysis method.** It investigates accidents and
  incidents across the full safety-control structure while guarding against
  hindsight bias and simplistic blame.
- **Safety must be designed and maintained.** The approach extends from early
  system engineering through operations, management, organizational culture,
  learning, and adaptation.

## Why it matters

The book shifts the central question from “Which component failed?” to “Why did
the system's controls fail to enforce the constraints required for safety?” That
change is especially useful where software, people, organizations, policy, and
physical processes interact in ways that cannot be reduced to independent
failure probabilities.
