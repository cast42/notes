# Mariana Mazzucato: culture as economic purpose

## TL;DR

Mariana Mazzucato argues that arts and culture should move from the margins to
the centre of economic thinking. Her follow-up reply makes the stronger claim:
doing that requires changing **what counts as value**, **how cultural activity
is funded**, and **who shares in the rewards it creates**.

The central new idea is a change in category. Culture is not just one sector,
or an instrument used to produce growth, health, education, or social cohesion.
It is part of the infrastructure through which an economy imagines its goals,
sets priorities, coordinates people, and decides what valuable outcomes are.

## The central idea

The familiar argument for public support of culture is instrumental: arts and
culture create positive spillovers such as tourism, jobs, skills, wellbeing,
social cohesion, or innovation. Those benefits can justify investment, but they
still leave culture downstream of an economic system whose goals and measures
are already fixed.

Mazzucato reverses that relationship. Culture helps constitute the system
upstream by shaping collective imagination and public purpose. It influences
which futures seem possible, which problems deserve attention, how people act
together, and what kinds of value institutions recognise. The implication is
not simply “spend more on the arts”; it is “include cultural capacity when
designing the economy and defining its success.”

## What changes if this is taken seriously

- **Valuation:** narrow market measures and short-term returns are insufficient
  for effects that are collective, contextual, nonlinear, and long term.
- **Funding:** culture should be treated as long-term public investment and
  social infrastructure, rather than a precarious discretionary cost justified
  project by project.
- **Economic purpose:** cultural activity helps societies imagine and debate the
  goals toward which economic capabilities are directed.
- **Distribution:** the question is not only how culture creates value, but who
  receives the value and power produced through it.
- **Governance:** cultural institutions and participation become part of
  mission-oriented policy, democratic legitimacy, and collective action.

## What is new—and what is not

It is not new to say that arts and culture support education, health, identity,
community, or innovation. The sharper contribution in this thread is to make
culture **constitutive rather than auxiliary**: it helps set the ends and value
criteria of the economy itself.

That is a promising reframing, but it remains a programme rather than a settled
economic theory. It raises practical questions about how cultural value should
be assessed, how institutions should be governed, how trade-offs are handled,
and how reward-sharing differs from ordinary public subsidy. The UCL PVAC
programme presents these as areas for research, partnerships, and policy
experimentation rather than as solved measurement problems.

## Connection to *The Common Good Economy*

The follow-up points to Mazzucato's *The Common Good Economy*, which argues that
economics should proactively shape markets toward collective goals rather than
only repair market or government failures afterward. The culture argument adds
an upstream layer to that project: before choosing instruments or missions,
societies need the imaginative and participatory capacities that help them
define the common good in the first place.
