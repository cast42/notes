# Cognitive Mercantilism

## TL;DR

Venkatesh Rao argues that some AI anxiety resembles mercantilism: it treats intelligence as a scarce stock to control and assumes most people are only marginal producers of it. Drawing on Joyce Appleby’s account of capitalism and the rise of new consumer demand, he offers a different possibility: cheap AI could let more people consume intelligence, pursue projects, and generate new demand for it. The historical analogy is suggestive, not proof that AI abundance will distribute opportunity or prosperity fairly.

## Key takeaways

- **Scarcity assumptions shape the debate.** Rao calls the view that intelligence is a fixed resource to be hoarded, priced, and controlled “cognitive mercantilism.” He contrasts it with fears that abundant AI will exceed society’s capacity to use it, which he calls “AI Malthusianism.”
- **Consumers need not also be producers.** The agricultural analogy is that most people can benefit from abundant food without farming. Similarly, people may use AI-enabled expertise without themselves producing specialist knowledge.
- **Demand can grow as costs fall.** Rao’s central hypothesis is that access to intelligence can make previously impractical interests and projects worth pursuing. The return on attention rises, potentially encouraging more voluntary cognitive effort—a “cognitive industrious revolution.”
- **The political question may shift.** Rather than only protecting existing cognitive jobs, society may need to consider who can access AI, pursue projects, and share in the value generated by an intelligence-rich economy.

## Argument and limits

Rao adapts Appleby’s account of capitalism’s contingent emergence and the creation of new consumer demand to think about AI. His analogy challenges a supply-only picture of intelligence: users are not merely passive recipients, and lower costs may create uses that were not previously economical or imaginable.

This is a historical analogy and a forecast, not evidence that AI demand is unlimited or that new opportunities will automatically appear. The essay’s optimistic mechanism depends on people having time, access, purchasing power, and institutional freedom to act on new possibilities. Greater aggregate demand or productivity would not by itself ensure that gains reach ordinary people. The risks of job displacement, concentrated ownership, and political control therefore remain live questions even if demand expands.

Appleby’s book itself is a broad, accessible synthesis, not an uncontested account of capitalism’s origins. Historian William Gallois praises its scope and narrative, while criticizing its Anglo-American emphasis and limited engagement with important international and critical scholarship. That qualification matters when Rao builds a contemporary analogy on it.
