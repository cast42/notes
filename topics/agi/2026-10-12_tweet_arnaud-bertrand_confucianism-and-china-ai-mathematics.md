# Confucian self-correction, pragmatic truth-seeking, and China's AI-mathematics initiative

## TL;DR

Arnaud Bertrand reads the launch of the Yau Laboratory Alliance for Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence as an example of Chinese elites publicly recognizing a capability gap and organizing to address it. He connects that response to a Confucian ideal of learning from one's insufficiency and to Deng Xiaoping's pragmatic “black cat, white cat” saying. The alliance and Yau's warning that China is behind in AI-assisted frontier science are independently reported; the claim that Confucian culture is a key cause of China's prospects is Bertrand's interpretation, not established evidence.

## The initiative and the argument

In October 2026, the Yau Laboratory Alliance for Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence was launched at Tsinghua University. Independent reporting says it brings together eight institutions and around 1,000 mathematicians to develop AI for mathematical research. Shing-Tung Yau reportedly acknowledged that China was behind the United States in using AI to advance frontier science. The initiative followed OpenAI's October 6 release of hundreds of AI-generated mathematical manuscripts; Bertrand presents the alliance as a response to that competitive moment.

Bertrand uses this example to challenge a picture of Chinese public discourse in which elites can only claim strength. He argues that acknowledging a gap can motivate coordinated action: pool talent, identify what is missing, and build capability. He then links this habit of self-correction to the *Xueji* (《学记》), a chapter of the *Book of Rites* (《礼记》), which says that learning reveals what one does not yet know and that recognizing one's limits enables reflection and self-strengthening.

## The “black cat, white cat” connection

Bertrand reports that Yau adapted Deng Xiaoping's famous cat aphorism for mathematical research: whatever the method or model, the result should be judged by whether it helps reach the truth. The well-known version associated with Deng is that a black or white cat is a good cat if it catches mice. In this context, the saying stands for pragmatic method selection and willingness to revise an approach when it fails—not loyalty to a label or ideology.

The analogy is suggestive but not a proof of cultural causation. A proverb can express an attractive norm without showing how widely it is practiced or whether it explains research performance. Nor does “judge by results” by itself specify safeguards for attribution, verification, openness, or responsible use of AI-generated mathematics.

## What the post supports—and what it does not

- **Corroborated event:** independent reports describe the alliance, its participating institutions, its scale, and Yau's concern about China's lag in AI-assisted mathematics.
- **Attributed cultural reading:** the Confucian connection and the particular “black cat, white cat” wording at the ceremony are reported here through Bertrand's post. The Chinese media pages linked by the post were not independently accessible during this review.
- **Unproven causal leap:** one example of candidly discussing a weakness does not establish that Confucian culture causes China's AI progress or guarantees the alliance's success. Talent concentration, research funding, compute, infrastructure, institutional coordination, and international competition are other plausible contributors that would need separate evidence.

The practical takeaway is narrower and more portable: state a capability gap clearly, treat learning as a way to discover what is missing, and choose tools by tested results. In research, keep “the result matters” paired with independent proof-checking and scientific norms.
