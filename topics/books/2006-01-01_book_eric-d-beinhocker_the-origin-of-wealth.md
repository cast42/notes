# The Origin of Wealth: Evolution, Complexity, and the Radical Remaking of Economics

## TL;DR

Eric D. Beinhocker argues that wealth is created by an evolving economic system,
not allocated by a machine that tends toward equilibrium. Physical technologies,
social technologies, and business designs interact in a **complex adaptive
system**. Their recurring process is **differentiate, select, and amplify**:
generate alternatives, test them against an environment, and expand the ones
that fit.

The book is valuable as a conceptual bridge between economics, evolutionary
theory, complexity science, and strategy. Its main limitation is also clear in
the reviews: it makes a large paradigm-level argument without supplying a
complete analytical replacement for neoclassical economics.

## Key takeaways

- **Wealth is fit order.** Beinhocker treats wealth as useful order that meets
  human needs and preferences, rather than only as money or a stock of assets.
- **The economy is open and out of equilibrium.** Innovation, institutions,
  networks, and human behaviour continually change the system, so equilibrium is
  a poor default picture of economic life.
- **Evolution is a search process.** Novelty comes from many experiments and
  combinations. Selection identifies what fits a changing environment, while
  amplification moves resources toward successful patterns.
- **Markets discover as well as allocate.** Their deeper role is to provide
  incentives, feedback, selection, and resource shifts during innovation—not
  merely to calculate an efficient equilibrium allocation.
- **Strategy should be a portfolio of experiments.** Organisations need to
  explore alternatives and learn, not only execute a single plan based on a
  forecast.
- **Institutions shape the fitness function.** Policy cannot direct every
  outcome, but it can influence which behaviours are rewarded, which experiments
  survive, and how quickly useful ideas spread.

## The argument in more detail

### From equilibrium economics to complexity economics

Beinhocker's target is the use of rational agents, perfect information, and
equilibrium as the default model of the economy. He does not claim that
neoclassical economics has produced no useful results; the stronger claim is
that those tools are badly matched to an economy that is dynamic, nonlinear,
networked, and continually generating novelty.

In the alternative picture, agents have bounded information and use heuristics.
Macroeconomic patterns emerge from interactions among agents rather than being
fully specified from above. The result is not necessarily optimal, but it can
be adaptive and productive.

### Technology, institutions, and business designs co-evolve

Economic evolution operates across several linked design spaces. New physical
technologies make new activities possible; social technologies—rules,
organisations, and institutions—coordinate people; and business designs combine
both into products and services. Wealth grows when this process finds better
ways to satisfy human purposes.

This is why Beinhocker's evolutionary language is more than a loose analogy:
the claim is about a recurring dynamic of variation, selection, replication, and
learning in economic systems. The analogy still needs care: economic agents can
anticipate, imitate, and deliberately run experiments, even though no central
designer controls the whole system.

### Implications for organisations and public policy

The practical recommendation is to increase **evolvability**: maintain enough
diversity for new options to appear, create feedback that can distinguish fit
from unfit options, and amplify promising results while cutting losses. Duncan
Green's application to NGOs makes the missing mechanism especially visible:
markets perform selection and amplification automatically, while public and
non-profit institutions must design those processes more deliberately.

## What reviewers found

- Herbert Gintis's *Journal of Economic Literature* review presents the book as a serious
  attack on neoclassical theory and argues that its lack of a finished
  analytical alternative is not a reason to dismiss it. The reviewer highlights
  dynamics, bounded agents, networks, emergence, and evolution as the book's
  useful organising ideas.
- *Publishers Weekly* calls Beinhocker an energetic and capable guide to the
  emerging complexity-economics paradigm, but says the book eventually tries to
  make one large idea explain too much.
- Eric Nehrlich appreciates the critique of neoclassical economics and the
  business-strategy sections, but finds the book a diffuse mixture of topics and
  doubts that its evolutionary model is developed enough to use as a practical
  theory.
- Duncan Green finds the evolutionary account useful for thinking about change
  in companies and NGOs. He also identifies a boundary: non-market social and
  political systems lack the market's obvious selection and amplification
  mechanism.

The balanced reading is therefore: **strong conceptual synthesis and a useful
systems lens; incomplete as a formal economic theory and uneven in its policy
extension.**
