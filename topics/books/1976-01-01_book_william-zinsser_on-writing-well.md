# On Writing Well: The Classic Guide to Writing Nonfiction

## TL;DR

William Zinsser’s guide treats nonfiction writing as a craft of clear thought and honest communication with a reader. Its recurring advice is to remove clutter, prefer direct and economical language, develop a voice that sounds like the writer, and revise until the reader can follow the point. Gergely Orosz recommends it as a classic resource for writing more concisely.

## Key takeaways

- **Clarity is an act of consideration.** Write so the reader can understand the point without fighting unnecessary wording or structure.
- **Cut clutter, not meaning.** Concision comes from deciding what matters and removing material that distracts from it, rather than merely shortening every sentence.
- **Use your own voice.** Zinsser encourages writers to express real ideas in their own words instead of hiding behind formulaic language or jargon.
- **Think of writing as a relationship.** The writer has to guide the reader and provide enough context to keep them oriented.
- **Learn by revising and reading examples.** Principles become usable through repeated practice and attention to how strong nonfiction works.

## Why it fits technical writing

Although the book is a general guide to nonfiction, its core practices transfer well to engineering work: proposals, design documents, incident reports, project updates, and explanations of technical decisions. These genres need accurate detail, but readers still benefit from an early statement of purpose, a clear account of who or what is affected, and an explanation of how the system or recommendation works.

That sequence—**why, who, how**—is a useful framing for such documents. It is Lode’s added advice, not a formula attributed to Zinsser.

## Reception and limits

HarperCollins presents the book as a broad guide to nonfiction, from writing about people and places to science, technology, business, and memoir. *Times Higher Education* praises its focus on directness, voice, and the writer’s responsibility to the reader. The original *Kirkus Reviews* notice similarly values its advice on directness and economy, while finding it less precise on some specialized journalistic techniques. A fair expectation is a set of durable craft principles and examples, not a comprehensive manual for every professional format.
