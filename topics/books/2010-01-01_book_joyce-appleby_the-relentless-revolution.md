# The Relentless Revolution: A History of Capitalism

## TL;DR

Joyce Appleby traces capitalism from its unlikely emergence in early-modern England to its global reach. She argues against treating it as a timeless or inevitable expression of human nature: specific changes in agriculture, trade, manufacturing, state power, and culture converged to create a system that generated wealth and ideas while also producing upheaval and exploitation.

## Key takeaways

- **Capitalism has a history.** It arose through particular circumstances, rather than appearing automatically wherever people trade or seek gain.
- **It is cultural as well as economic.** Changes in ideas about work, wealth, consumption, and human possibility helped make a new economic system viable.
- **The state and markets have interacted.** Appleby’s account includes states harnessing and extending capitalist practices, rather than reducing capitalism to laissez-faire markets.
- **Creation and destruction coexist.** The system creates wealth, power, and new ideas, while generating disruption and oppression.
- **Demand matters to the transition.** Rao’s later essay draws on Appleby’s account of ordinary people adopting new consumer tastes and increasing their work and initiative in response to newly available opportunities.

## Reception and qualification

W. W. Norton presents the book as an accessible global introduction and quotes positive notices from *The New Yorker*, *The Seattle Times*, *Choice*, and the *Fort Worth Star-Telegram*. Historian William Gallois likewise praises its accessible prose, broad scope, and treatment of capitalism as a cultural system. He argues, however, that its largely Anglo-American viewpoint and limited engagement with major international and critical scholarship make it less satisfying for specialists than for general readers. The book is best read as an ambitious interpretive synthesis, not a final consensus account of capitalism’s origins.
