# System Engineering Design

## TL;DR

Rogier Wolfert, Ruud Binnekamp, Harold van Heukelum and Yue Shang introduce a preference-based approach to engineering design: express stakeholder objectives and feasible physical constraints, then search for a configuration that best fits the group's modeled preferences. The aim is to define the right problem before settling on a solution.

The book was shared by [Andriy Burkov](https://x.com/burkov/status/2122870656757026963), who links to a [ChapterPal edition](https://www.chapterpal.com/book/2055ecbe-34f1-4a0c-b71e-988bec1dbd50/system-engineering-design). Its [source chapters are available on GitHub](https://github.com/TeachBooks/systems-engineering-design/tree/main/book/chapters).

## Key ideas

- **Needs precede artifacts.** Beginning with a preferred technical object can produce an answer to the wrong problem; identify the affected stakeholders and the needs their design should serve.
- **Preferences and feasibility are different.** Model what people value alongside the physical or other constraints that limit possible configurations.
- **Design the solution space, not just the shortlist.** The authors contrast choosing among preselected alternatives with building preferences and constraints into a search for a suitable configuration.
- **Design and science interact.** The book distinguishes imagining feasible things that do not yet exist from empirical investigation of the existing world; modeling and simulation help assess candidate designs.
- **Use a systems view.** Zooming out to the larger system and its context complements analysis of individual parts.

## What the book covers

The accessible introduction describes foundations in engineering design, research, modeling and simulation, decision-making, systems thinking, and optimization, followed by a preference-based methodology and applications. The GitHub table of contents currently marks some later chapters as excluded from publication; their presence in the repository does not establish that every edition is complete.

The introduction argues that common preference scales and weighted arithmetic aggregation can be problematic. These are the authors' methodological claims; this note does not independently evaluate them. A computed group optimum also depends on whose preferences are represented, how they are measured and aggregated, and whether constraints and models match reality. It does not by itself settle questions of fairness or legitimacy.

## Why it may be useful

The framing offers a structured way to examine infrastructure or organizational choices where technical feasibility and differing stakeholder objectives interact. For example, before comparing electricity-grid plans, one could make the stakeholders, desired outcomes, constraints, and assumptions about the future explicit. This is an illustrative application, not a case study verified in the book.
