# Research capture: *The Origin of Wealth*

## Source notes

### Harvard Business Review Press

The publisher describes the book as a complexity-economics account of how
wealth emerges in a self-organising economy. Its central formula is
“differentiate, select, and amplify”. It connects physical technologies, social
technologies, and business designs, and applies the framework to organisations,
markets, and public policy.

Source: [HBR Press](https://store.hbr.org/product/the-origin-of-wealth-evolution-complexity-and-the-radical-remaking-of-economics/777X)

### Journal of Economic Literature — Herbert Gintis

The review presents the book as a frontal challenge to neoclassical economics,
but says the absence of a workable analytical replacement should not lead
readers to dismiss it. It identifies five organising differences: dynamics,
bounded agents, networks, emergence, and evolution. It also treats markets as
an evolutionary search mechanism rather than only an equilibrium allocator.

Source: [Journal of Economic Literature review](https://www.umass.edu/preferen/gintis/Beinhocker%20JEL%202006.pdf)

### Publishers Weekly

The review praises the book as an able guide to a new economic paradigm and
describes its account of open, adaptive systems and interacting technologies,
social development, and business practice. Its criticism is that the book's
large idea expands toward a cure-all, especially in the political and social
claims.

Source: [Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781578517770)

### Eric Nehrlich

Nehrlich values the critique of rational-agent and equilibrium assumptions and
some of the strategy and finance arguments. He criticises the book as a loose
mixture of complexity theory, economics, strategy, and politics, and doubts that
the evolutionary model is sufficiently developed to guide analysis.

Source: [Eric Nehrlich, “The Origin of Wealth”](https://www.nehrlich.com/blog/2007/09/14/the-origin-of-wealth-by-eric-d-beinhocker/)

### Duncan Green

Green applies the book's evolutionary view of change to NGOs and social
institutions. He finds the ideas useful for designing experimentation,
selection, and learning, but points out that non-market systems lack the
market's built-in selection and amplification signals.

Source: [Duncan Green, “Complexity Economics, Evolution and How Change Happens”](https://frompoverty.oxfam.org.uk/complexity-economics-evolution-and-how-change-happens/)
