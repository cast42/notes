# Hagakure: The Book of the Samurai

## TL;DR

*Hagakure* (“In the Shadow of Leaves”) is a collection of reflections dictated by retired samurai Yamamoto Tsunetomo to the younger retainer Tashiro Tsuramoto between 1709 and 1716. It is not a systematic treatise: the sayings range across service, conduct, training, attention, and readiness for death. Ed Latimore’s linked article selects passages and reframes them as practical advice for modern self-development.

## What Latimore draws from it

Latimore’s 2021 article highlights themes that can travel beyond the original setting: learn from people with experience rather than endlessly reinventing solutions; distinguish useful action from easy criticism; correct others with tact; treat skill as a practice that calls for humility; allow for growth after mistakes; and give attention to the present task. These are Latimore’s contemporary readings of selected passages, not a complete account of the book.

One especially modern overlay is the four-stage “unconscious incompetence → conscious incompetence → conscious competence → unconscious competence” model. Latimore maps a passage about levels of study onto that framework; the mapping should not be mistaken for Tsunetomo’s own terminology.

## Historical context and limits

The text was dictated in the Saga domain in the early eighteenth century. Columbia University’s teaching resource describes it as spirited reflections rather than a rigorous philosophical exposition and notes that it was not widely circulated during the Tokugawa period outside the Saga domain. Its later reputation as a defining expression of *bushidō* grew much later, including its embrace by Japanese nationalists in the 1930s.

That history matters when reading its severe language about death and loyalty. The work speaks from the obligations and ideals of a particular warrior-retainer world; it is not a timeless handbook for everyone. Modern motivational adaptations can make selected ideas useful, but they should not erase that context or imply that every passage supports present-day self-help advice.

The Shambhala edition translated by William Scott Wilson selects 300 representative pieces from the more than 1,300 short texts described by the publisher. Ed Latimore’s article does not identify its translation, so the wording of his selected quotations should not automatically be attributed to Wilson’s edition.
