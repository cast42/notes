# Co-Intelligence — what reviewers are saying

*Ethan Mollick*

## TL;DR

- Reviewers broadly praise the book as an accessible, practical introduction to human–AI collaboration and a useful starting point for newcomers.
- The recurring criticism is that Mollick’s optimism outruns the book’s evidence and treatment of ethical, educational, environmental, and social risks.
- The Simon Sinek interview reinforces the book’s emphasis on experimentation, human judgment, experience, taste, voice, and agency, but is a later sympathetic conversation rather than independent review evidence.

## Highlights

- Bring AI to the table, but remain the human in the loop.
- A conversation starter and accessible introduction—not a definitive research foundation.
- The jagged frontier: AI is uneven, so learn its fit through use and verification.

## What reviewers are saying

The positive consensus is about usefulness and accessibility, not technical novelty. Kirkus calls it an authoritative, jargon-free roadmap. Christoph Pöppe (Spektrum der Wissenschaft) values its practical rules: invite AI, define its role, and remain responsible for the result. Christopher Eaton’s academic review says the book is strongest on workplace and educational use, and that its examples can help readers understand how AI complements human thinking.

The recurring reservations are substantive:

- Eaton finds too little data, theoretical grounding, and analysis of how AI-generated language can shape a writer’s own ideas and long-term learning. He sees the book as a useful conversation starter, but not a durable research foundation.
- Eden Elizabeth Armstrong praises its clarity, AI-literacy emphasis, and human oversight, while criticising the optimism around safety, equity, sustainability, and risk. Experimentation is less transferable to high-stakes, risk-averse settings.
- The Education Review assessment says the book’s practical rules are valuable but that its ethical and social answers remain incomplete. It also warns that treating AI as a partner can encourage anthropomorphism and obscure the systems’ lack of judgment.
- Pöppe’s review similarly stresses that fluent output can conceal errors; “human in the loop” means checking and taking responsibility, not merely adding a human at the end.

Overall, the reviews support reading *Co-Intelligence* as a highly accessible orientation and experimentation guide. They do not support treating it as a settled theory of intelligence, a technical account of AI, or a sufficient treatment of the societal consequences.

## What the video adds

Simon Sinek’s 16 June 2026 interview with Mollick, [The AI Skills Nobody is Teaching (And Everyone Needs)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9YMYVb1ASCg), updates the practical message for a later AI landscape. Its emphasis is on agency, experience, taste, personal voice, learning, privacy, and the “jagged frontier” of uneven model capability. The video argues that human experience and judgment are not merely leftovers after automation; they are part of the capability needed to use AI well.

That is a useful extension of the book’s “bring AI to the table” principle, but the video is a sympathetic interview and its description/chapter list—not an independent review or a transcript-based analysis. It also points forward to Mollick’s later book *Co-Existence*, so claims about that later phase should not be retroactively attributed to *Co-Intelligence*.

## Assessment

The book remains useful as a starting point for people who need a practical mental model for working with generative AI: experiment across tasks, learn the jagged frontier, define the system’s role, verify outputs, and keep responsibility with the human. Its weakness is the gap between that strong operating advice and the thinner evidence base for broader claims about education, work, ethics, and social outcomes.

The most durable reading is therefore conditional: use the book to generate experiments and questions, not to settle whether AI improves thinking or learning. Measure the outcome, inspect what the human still understands, and account for the risks that the positive case may underweight.
