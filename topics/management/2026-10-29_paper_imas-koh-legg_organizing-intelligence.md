# Organizing Intelligence: competence, coordination, and economic transformation

## TL;DR

Alex Imas, Andrew Koh, and Shane Legg model how AI capability changes firms, wages, employment, and market structure. The key distinction is between **competence**—how well an agent performs individual tasks—and **coordination**—how well agents combine their work as an organization grows. Competence can raise output without changing the limits on firm size; sufficiently strong coordination could change those limits sharply. These are model-based scenarios, not empirical forecasts.

The three sources fit together: Alex Imas’s X post announces the project; the DeepMind Institute essay gives an accessible account; the paper *Organizing Intelligence* supplies the formal economic model.

## The paper’s argument

- **Organizations are the economically relevant unit.** A model that beats each person in isolated tests may still lose to a human team whose members have complementary capabilities.
- **Human teams can build “mosaic intelligence.”** People have uneven strengths in different domains; firms can combine specialists so that one person’s gaps are covered by another’s strengths. Reproducing the same AI agent adds capacity, but may not add capability breadth.
- **Competence and coordination have different effects.** Greater task competence raises productive capacity. Greater coordination makes output scale better with team size. The former can change wages and the human/AI mix while leaving typical firm size largely bounded; the latter could raise the efficient scale of organizations.
- **A coordination threshold could reshape markets.** In the model, if coordination becomes strong enough, adding members can keep increasing productivity. This can break the Coasean ceiling on firm size and create a phase transition toward large, concentrated AI-heavy firms.
- **Labor-market effects need not wait for visible reorganization.** Wages and roles may respond as AI substitutes for some tasks or changes the value of coordinators, before market concentration or employment patterns shift substantially.

The paper distinguishes four capability regimes: minimal AGI, supercompetent AI, supercooperative AI, and ASI. These are analytical scenarios organized along competence and coordination—not a claim that current systems have crossed those thresholds.

## The essay and its illustrative evidence

The DeepMind Institute essay presents the framework in accessible terms and reports an exercise in which 100 people took a quiz across six domains. The authors say that pairs selected for complementary strengths performed 32% better than randomly paired participants on the task. This illustrates the potential value of complementarity in human teams; it is not a demonstration of AI-team performance or a direct estimate of economy-wide productivity.

## Limits and reading stance

This is a formal theoretical model. The authors explicitly say its scenarios are outcomes under model assumptions, not empirical forecasts of market outcomes or technological developments. The paper therefore identifies mechanisms and thresholds worth measuring; it does not establish when, or whether, AI will reach them. Coordination is a key empirical unknown, and the paper argues that current single-agent benchmarks do not measure it well.

The DeepMind Institute describes its essays as conversation starters reflecting their authors’ ideas, not Google’s official view. The paper likewise says it is for academic discussion and does not necessarily reflect its authors’ affiliated institutions.
