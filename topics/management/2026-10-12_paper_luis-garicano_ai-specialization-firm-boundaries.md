# AI, Smith, and Coase: the vanishing advantage of specialization

## TL;DR

Luis Garicano's Brookings paper asks how AI changes which work firms do themselves and which they buy from specialists. If AI makes reliable knowledge cheaper to acquire, firms can bring more tasks in-house; if it makes finding and contracting with outside providers cheaper, outsourcing can grow instead. These are competing “Smith” (division of labor) and “Coase” (firm-boundary) forces. The paper presents early evidence consistent with the knowledge-cost mechanism, but explicitly says it is preliminary and not a causal test.

## The paper's mechanism

Specialists have an advantage because they learn something once and reuse it across many clients. That spreads the fixed cost of acquiring expertise. An occasional user—a worker or a company that faces a problem less often—usually finds it cheaper to ask a specialist than to maintain the same knowledge itself.

Garicano argues that AI can lower the fixed cost of acquiring and maintaining reliable knowledge. That weakens the specialist's scale advantage: firms may internalize recurring problems that previously went to outside law, accounting, software, consulting, or marketing providers. Specialists would then focus on rarer and harder problems, while general reusable knowledge may be supplied upstream by model makers.

But AI can also reduce transaction costs: search, matching, communication, and contracting with outside experts. That mechanism points the other way, toward more market purchases and outsourcing. Which force dominates depends on how fast each cost falls, as well as how often the firm and outside specialist encounter each kind of problem. A larger market can itself strengthen specialist economies of scale.

## What the evidence says so far

The conference draft brings together several suggestive signals:

- In a sample of work-related ChatGPT messages, 43.5% of occupation-specific messages concerned tasks usually associated with another occupation. This indicates that people use AI to cross occupational boundaries; it does not directly show whether firms insource a task.
- A Procter & Gamble field experiment found that individuals working alone with AI could produce proposals of similar quality to teams working without AI. This is evidence that AI can let a person reach outside their own function, not a direct estimate of long-run organizational change.
- In-house legal teams report rising generative-AI use and expect less reliance on outside providers for routine tasks. These are survey expectations, not observed purchases.
- U.S. labor statistics show falling shares of some professional occupations employed in their main specialist industries after 2022. The author calls this consistent with the mechanism, but the pattern alone cannot establish that AI caused the shift.

Garicano is explicit about the limitation: the paper's evidence is early and indicative. It does not directly observe the buyer's in-house production, the frequency of each problem before AI, or all changes in demand and firm circumstances. A stronger causal test would track one well-defined task and who takes responsibility for it before and after AI adoption, with a comparison group.

## Organizational and career implications

- **The specialist's edge may move up and down the chain.** Model providers can supply general knowledge at scale; client firms can apply it to recurring problems using their local context. Experts retain an advantage where expertise remains difficult to acquire, problems are rare, or clients need accountable verification.
- **The “make or buy” decision is about problem frequency, not just company size.** A large company can still outsource an unusual merger or other one-off task, while a smaller firm may internalize a problem it encounters often.
- **Apprenticeship may lose its training substrate.** Routine junior work both finances professional training and gives novices cases on which to learn. If AI removes that work, firms and schools may need new ways to build expertise. Specialists may still be needed for exceptional cases and checking, but juniors cannot instantly learn by doing only exceptional cases.
- **Policy matters.** The paper highlights licensing, competition, and training: who may provide or sign off on a service; whether model providers can restrict access or charge high fixed fees; and how professional expertise will be developed.

## My reading

Mateos Garcia's post captures the paper's main contrast in a few lines: AI could reduce specialization by making knowledge cheaper to internalize, or increase it by making transactions cheaper. The paper's contribution is to make these forces empirically distinguishable rather than assume that “AI lowers costs” has one organizational consequence.

The provocative thesis is not that specialists disappear. Their work may become more concentrated in the rare, difficult, high-consequence tail, with value shifting toward context, judgment, accountability, and verification. The career-system question is as important as the headcount question: if routine tasks vanish, where will the next generation acquire expertise?

This complements [Hemeon and Jack's argument about AI changing information routing and hierarchy](2026-03-31_x_hemeon_jack_from-hierarchy-to-intelligence.md): Garicano focuses on the boundary between internal work and outside suppliers, while that note focuses on coordination layers inside organizations.
