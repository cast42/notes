# Messy Jobs: Thomas Wolf's 15 cards on AI and work

## TL;DR

Thomas Wolf's cards present his reading of *Messy Jobs: The Work That AI Cannot Reach* by Luis Garicano, Jin Li and Yanhui Wu. Their central lens is that a job is a **bundle of tasks**, not one capability: AI's effect depends on how tasks fit together, what remains scarce, demand, institutions, and organizational adaptation. The cards combine data, examples, interpretation and forecasts; they are not a guarantee that any occupation is safe.

## The 15 cards

1. **Shifting scarcity.** Historical improvements in lighting drastically lowered the work time needed to buy light. Wolf argues that each technological wave changes which capabilities are scarce; for AI, he highlights trust and accountability. Scarcity matters to wages, but the card's contrast with productivity is a simplification: bargaining power, institutions and demand matter too.
2. **Satiation versus expanding demand.** Farm employment shrank as productivity rose and food demand was limited; cheaper steam power coincided with greater coal use. Which pattern applies to AI-enabled analysis, code or advice remains an empirical question, possibly different for each use.
3. **Capability is not adoption.** Elevator operators initially grew in number despite automatic elevators. Wolf highlights demand for human service and regulation, plus present-day obstacles including verification, experiential learning and physical presence. Timing and historical causation are more complex than the card's single strike explanation.
4. **Strong and weak task bundles.** A nurse's interdependent tasks may stay bundled even if notes are automated; a billing clerk's more separable tasks may be unbundled. These are schematic examples, not measured predictions for individual jobs.
5. **Radiologists as an example.** The card contrasts growth in AI radiology devices with ongoing radiology training, and points to procedures, consultations and patient context beyond image reading. Device clearance and residency places are not measures of AI effectiveness or proof of future job security; the workday breakdown cited comes from a small 2012 study.
6. **Who captures the gains?** In the card's schematic, widely shared tools can favor stars and niches while squeezing the local middle. For workers with strong bundles, demand elasticity and entry barriers influence whether productivity translates into higher pay. This is a model, not an established forecast.
7. **Human agreement and authority.** Computing may accelerate while negotiation, trust, liability and legitimate decision-making remain bottlenecks. Wolf argues that human authority retains value; whether and where authority can be delegated is institutional and context-dependent, not a settled impossibility theorem.
8. **A human premium.** Some buyers may value verifiable, perceptible or expressive human involvement, as with live performance. Cheaper automated goods could free spending for human services; the magnitude and distribution of that premium are uncertain.
9. **Implementation needs three kinds of knowledge.** Successful organizational change combines knowledge of actual work, technology and internal politics. Wolf suggests finding people who bridge all three; his examples of fast deployment and status incentives are claims to test, not universal prescriptions.
10. **Individual uptake versus organizational transformation.** People can adopt AI tools faster than firms redesign interconnected processes. Complementary changes, uncertainty about new workflows and resistance can slow transformation; uptake of tools alone is not evidence of productivity gains.
11. **Hiring signals change.** If AI makes polished application materials easier for many candidates, they may distinguish capability less well. The cards suggest track records, substantive tasks and conversation as alternatives; those signals have their own biases and costs.
12. **Workslop and verification.** Generating a polished draft can be cheap while checking it remains costly, shifting work to reviewers. Proposed responses include smaller trust units, visible processes and rewarding verification. The chart is illustrative, not a measured estimate.
13. **The junior pipeline.** Automating entry-level tasks may weaken routes to experience. The cited drop in hiring at large tech firms is a descriptive comparison, not proof that AI caused it. The cards suggest deliberate learning, personal projects, smaller teams and combining skills; employers also need training pathways.
14. **The mediocrity trap.** AI may deliver an acceptable-looking draft quickly, but reaching high quality still requires judgment and iteration. The 90% curve is schematic. The cited 2025 METR experiment concerned a specific group and tools; its authors later cautioned that the old result does not measure current tools.
15. **Growth can meet slow-sector bottlenecks.** A stylized two-sector model illustrates how a rapidly improving sector's spending weight can shrink while slower human services dominate. The numerical trajectory depends on assumptions about productivity and demand, not a prediction. Problem selection, implementation and slow sectors could constrain aggregate gains.

## What to take from it

Ask not just “Can AI do this task?” but: which tasks share context, responsibility or learning; who verifies the result; and does cheaper output expand demand? Treat the cards as a useful framework and set of hypotheses. The underlying book and each cited dataset deserve separate examination before relying on individual numbers or strong causal claims. This note is based on Wolf's 15 cards and the book's public description, not a reading of the whole book.
