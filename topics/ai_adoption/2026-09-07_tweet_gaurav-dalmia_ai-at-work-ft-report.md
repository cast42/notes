# The best, worst and strangest ways AI is being used at work

*Financial Times — shared by Gaurav Dalmia (@gdalmiathinks)*

## TL;DR

- The FT image presents workplace AI as a mixed operational reality: it speeds up research, document handling, analysis, and drug discovery, but also creates hallucination, privacy, over-reliance, emissions, and governance risks.
- The most valuable uses often improve the **inputs to professional work**—searching institutional knowledge, summarising client information, exploring alternatives, and identifying patterns—rather than simply automating the final deliverable.
- The examples suggest that AI adoption is uneven by profession: consulting and banking use it to compress preparation; lawyers use it to search contracts but face court-filing failures; pharma sees large potential but remains constrained by regulation and safety.

## Highlights from the image

- **Consulting:** internal tools make historical slide decks, project lessons, and proprietary market data searchable; McKinsey consultants can receive the results as an interactive bespoke podcast. A stranger use is helping assemble project teams by technical skills, expertise, and personal “chemistry.”
- **Oil and gas:** AI is used for non-disclosure agreements, operational dashboards, seismic interpretation, drilling trajectories, and convenience-store stock management. A stated drawback is that productivity gains may increase emissions and complicate net-zero goals.
- **Banking:** internal models and services such as JPMorgan’s LLM Suite and Rogo summarise client information and takeover targets, and help draft financial models and presentations. Risks include loss of institutional feel for numbers and obviously unedited AI emails.
- **Law:** AI makes contract analysis faster and enables deeper review, but hallucinated citations and sensitive-data restrictions can increase risk, staffing, and court friction. A strange use is an AI-drafted amicus brief in a Trump lawsuit.
- **Pharmaceuticals:** AI may shorten molecule discovery and help identify biomarkers, but regulation, bias, false recommendations, and biosecurity remain serious constraints. The image also describes research where LLM-generated “hallucinations” improved drug-discovery performance.

## What the article illustrates

The page is a useful field snapshot rather than a clean productivity case. AI often removes low-status, time-consuming work, which can free professionals for judgment—but it can also increase the amount of analysis expected, shift errors into high-stakes documents, and create pressure to demonstrate AI use even when the use is performative.

The recurring pattern is **augmentation under verification**: organizations gain speed and broader search, but the output still requires domain judgment, privacy controls, and checking against reality. In regulated settings, the cost of a plausible error can erase the efficiency gain.
