# The World is Changing: AI For Creativity — Jeffrey Katzenberg

*Jeffrey Katzenberg — @jeffreykWNDR*

## TL;DR

- AI can expand the creative canvas, but the tools do not replace taste, imagination, empathy or the human reason for making a story.
- Katzenberg frames Hollywood’s resistance as love of craft and compares the transition with earlier disruptions such as recorded sound and computer animation.
- The practical settlement is not tools versus no tools, but terms: credit, consent, compensation and accountability for the creative work that trains and shapes the systems.
- He distinguishes reasoning from creating and argues that today’s AI is far stronger at evaluating patterns than at originating human creative intention.
- The essay’s optimistic forecast is conditional: more creative output depends on building AI with storytellers, not on top of them.

## Highlights

- To resist technology is to risk irrelevance.
- The north has the new tools. The south has the creative soul.
- Katzenberg’s 90% production-cost claim is a personal forecast, not an independently verified result.

## Core idea

Katzenberg’s argument is a conditional optimism about AI and creative work. AI may lower the cost of animation and open a much larger creative canvas, but tools alone do not make a story matter. Human taste, imagination, emotional judgment and the desire to communicate remain the scarce ingredients in his account.

He treats artists’ resistance as evidence of commitment rather than simple technophobia. People fight hard because the work is a calling, and because the transition can remove livelihoods even when the surrounding art form grows.

## The historical analogy

The essay compares generative AI with two earlier transitions:

- The phonograph threatened composers’ income and helped provoke a change in copyright terms. The technology remained; the economic rules changed.
- Recorded sound removed many live musicians’ jobs in cinema, but also enabled musicals, modern scoring, sound design and a larger medium.
- Computer animation replaced parts of hand-drawn production while enabling new forms of scale, movement and visual expression. Katzenberg’s Disney and DreamWorks experience makes this history personal rather than abstract.

The useful lesson is not that every technological disruption turns out well. It is that “technology versus no technology” is an incomplete question. The distribution of losses, gains and creative control depends on the terms of adoption.

## Reasoning versus creating

Katzenberg draws a sharp distinction between reasoning and creating. In his framing, reasoning evaluates facts, premises and evidence toward a constrained conclusion. Creating generates something that did not exist before, where taste, intuition and vision select among many possible answers.

He argues that current AI is much stronger at pattern-matching, evaluation and optimisation than at human creative intention. This is a useful distinction for the note, but it remains an argument rather than a settled scientific boundary. Models can generate novel combinations and useful artistic material; the harder question is whether they have a human-like reason for making a choice or communicating something to an audience.

## A negotiated path forward

The proposed settlement has four parts:

- **Build with storytellers:** artists should shape the tools and workflows, not merely supply training material or absorb the disruption.
- **Credit:** creative contribution and provenance should remain visible.
- **Consent:** creators should have a say in whether and how their work is used.
- **Compensation and accountability:** economic value and responsibility should follow the creative pipeline.

Katzenberg’s optimistic forecast—more films, more experimentation and new forms—therefore depends on institutions solving the terms of adoption. The essay is not a neutral forecast: Katzenberg has strong incentives to emphasise opportunity, and his 90% cost-reduction claim is presented as a personal prediction rather than evidence from a controlled study.

## My notes

The strongest idea is the separation between **capability expansion** and **value allocation**. A tool can enlarge what is technically possible while still making the transition unjust for the people whose craft, data and judgment made the new system valuable. “Human creativity” is not a sufficient governance principle unless it becomes concrete rules for consent, credit, compensation and control.

The essay also gives a useful design test: if AI is supposed to amplify creativity, ask what creative decision remains human, who gets to make it, and who bears the cost when the workflow changes.
