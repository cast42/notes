# How to stay smart in the age of AI: the science of critical thinking

*Helen Pearson*

## TL;DR

- AI can improve assisted performance while weakening unaided learning if it removes the cognitive work through which skills develop.
- Critical thinking is domain-dependent: it requires subject knowledge plus practice with reasoning, alternatives and evidence.
- The strongest practical distinction is between AI that elicits thinking and AI that substitutes for it; the overall evidence is not yet settled.

## Highlights

- Performance gains during AI use are not the same as learning.
- Teach critical thinking inside subjects, with domain knowledge and varied practice.
- Test unaided transfer before treating AI-supported performance as durable skill.

## The central distinction

The article is most useful as a warning against equating better performance
with better learning. AI can help someone complete a task while reducing the
memory, reasoning and problem-solving work that builds unaided capability. The
relevant design question is whether the tool elicits that work through hints,
feedback and alternatives, or lets the learner bypass it with a ready-made
answer.

## What the evidence supports

- Critical thinking is not a single context-free skill. It depends on domain
  knowledge and on practice with the reasoning moves that matter in that field.
- The evidence on generative AI and learning is mixed and still too young for a
  universal verdict. Survey concerns are not causal estimates, and short-term
  performance gains are not measures of durable skill.
- Bastani and colleagues' field experiment is a concrete caution: unrestricted
  GPT-4 access improved practice performance but students later performed worse
  without the tool; a tutor with learning-oriented guardrails mitigated that
  effect without producing a clear positive transfer benefit.
- A sensible evaluation should include unaided transfer, not only output quality
  while the assistant is available.

## Teaching implications

The specialists quoted by Pearson recommend embedding critical-thinking practice
inside subjects, building the relevant knowledge base, and giving students
varied problems that require them to consider alternatives and evidence. This
supports a practical rule for AI-assisted learning: preserve some effortful
thinking in the workflow, and test what remains when the tool is removed.

## Limits

This is a Nature news feature, not a systematic review or a new experiment. It
reports expert views, survey results and selected studies; the overall causal
effect of generative AI on learning and critical thinking remains unresolved.
