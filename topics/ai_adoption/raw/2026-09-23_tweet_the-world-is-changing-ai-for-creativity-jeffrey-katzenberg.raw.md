Core argument:
Katzenberg argues that AI will expand the creative canvas, but that the future
of creative work depends on combining Silicon Valley’s tools with Hollywood’s
storytelling judgment. He treats resistance from artists as evidence of care
for the craft, not as simple technophobia.

Main claims and examples:
- He recalls seeing a striking AI-generated animated scene and compares the
  experience with the first time he saw computer animation come alive in Luxo
  Jr.
- He repeats his 2023 prediction that AI could reduce the time and cost of
  producing world-class animation by as much as 90% within three years; this
  is his own forecast, not an independently verified result.
- He compares AI resistance with earlier conflicts around the phonograph and
  recorded sound. His lesson is that disrupted jobs can disappear even while
  the art form expands, and that the decisive issue is the terms of adoption:
  consent, compensation and recognition of creative work.
- Drawing on Walt Disney, Pixar and the transition from hand-painted cels to
  CGI, he argues that tools are not the essence of animation. Taste,
  imagination, character and the audience’s emotional response are.
- He distinguishes reasoning from creating: reasoning evaluates existing
  premises toward a constrained answer, while creation makes choices where
  no single correct answer is already implied. He says current AI is much
  stronger at the former than at human creative intention.
- His proposed path is collaboration by design: build with storytellers, not
  on top of them, with credit, consent and compensation.
- He predicts more films, more experimentation and new storytelling forms as
  costs and barriers fall, while insisting that the terms of the transition
  are a political and economic choice.

Short excerpts:
“To resist technology is to risk irrelevance.”
“The north has the new tools. The south has the creative soul.”
