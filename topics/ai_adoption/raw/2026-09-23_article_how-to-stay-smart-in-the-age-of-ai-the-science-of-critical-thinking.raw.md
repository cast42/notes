Core question

The feature asks how people can preserve memory, reasoning, problem-solving and
other forms of higher-order thinking when generative AI makes it easy to
delegate intellectual work. The concern is not that every use of AI damages
learning. It is that a tool can improve performance on an assisted task while
removing the cognitive processing through which durable learning occurs.

What the feature reports

- Education researcher Lixiang Yan found that an agentic AI could complete a
  course's assignments, answer questions and participate in discussions without
  the learner being intellectually engaged.
- The article cites a 2025 survey in which 90% of more than 1,000 US faculty
  respondents expected generative AI to reduce students' critical-thinking
  skills. It also cites a global survey in which about 20% of more than 8,000
  student respondents who use AI said they find it harder to work through
  problems without it. These are survey perceptions, not causal estimates.
- Cognitive psychologist Daniel Willingham describes learning as a consequence
  of cognitive processing and notes that AI can remove effort from that process.
- The feature distinguishes critical thinking from one generic skill. It is a
  bundle of reasoning, memory, decision-making and problem-solving processes,
  and effective performance depends on knowledge of the relevant domain.
- A 2024 study discussed in the feature found that students with access to a
  ChatGPT-like tool solved mathematics problems better while the tool was
  available but performed worse after it was removed than students who never
  had access. The underlying PNAS field experiment is linked below.
- The evidence base is still unsettled: tools differ, studies conflict, and
  too few rigorous long-term studies exist to establish the overall effect of
  generative AI on learning or critical thinking.
- AI can support learning when it gives feedback, prompts or tasks that require
  students to think and solve problems. In practice, however, learners often
  prefer quick answers and may switch away from deliberately scaffolded tools.
- The Education Endowment Foundation announced up to £2.5 million for rigorous
  studies of generative AI's effects on learning, understanding, memory and
  problem-solving.

Teaching implications

The specialists interviewed argue that critical thinking should be taught
inside subjects rather than as a free-floating skill. Students need relevant
domain knowledge and repeated practice on varied problems. Teaching should make
reasoning moves explicit, such as corroborating records in history or relating
theory, hypothesis, method and evidence in science. A coherent curriculum can
build knowledge and reasoning over time.

The article also stresses an evidence problem: critical thinking is harder to
measure consistently than mathematics or reading, so claims about what works
need better assessments and longer-term comparisons.

Interpretive boundary

The feature supports a design question rather than a universal rule that AI is
bad for thinking. The relevant distinction is between assistance that preserves
or elicits cognitive work and substitution that lets the learner bypass it.
Performance gains during tool use should not be treated as evidence of learning
unless unaided transfer is also tested.
